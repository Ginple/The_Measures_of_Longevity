Abstract
Microenvironment remodelling impacts tumour growth and metastasis, but whether remodelling promotes pre-malignant clonal fitness remains unknown. Here, using single-cell RNA-sequencing of the bone-marrow microenvironment in a mouse model of
DNMT3A
-mutant clonal haematopoiesis (CH), we identify mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) in a molecular state of cellular senescence. Elevated bone-marrow MSC senescence is also observed in humans with CH driven by several common somatic mutations. MSC senescence is induced by mutant haematopoietic cells in a contact-independent manner through production of soluble factors including TNF-α and IL-6. These cytokines activate a Stat3-driven pathway that is necessary and sufficient for MSC senescence induction. Genetic or pharmacological depletion of senescent non-haematopoietic cells reduces the burden of CH and delays progression to myeloid neoplasia. Our findings show that microenvironment remodelling modifies pre-malignant clonal fitness and identifies disruption of the crosstalk between pre-malignant cells and their niche as a cancer prevention strategy.

Hematopoietic Stem Cell Biology and Microenvironment Dynamics
Main
Through the course of a lifetime, tissues evolve by an iterative process of genetic diversification, clonal selection, and clonal expansion within the adaptive context of tissue environments
1
. This plasticity provides resilience of multicellular tissues to the perturbations and stress that impair their function but also serves as the foundation for potential cancer clonal evolution and malignant transformation if not tightly regulated. In the haematopoietic system, ageing is associated with a functional decline of haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs)
2
. Around 20% of aged individuals will develop clonal haematopoiesis (CH)
3
, in which HSCs carrying adaptive somatic mutations are positively selected in the bone marrow (BM) microenvironment. CH is a non-malignant clonal state and can provide beneficial effects to functional T-cell immunity in ageing
4
; however, several of the adaptive somatic mutations underlying CH are found in clones that evolve into blood cancers
5
. CH can also infiltrate and remodel the solid tumour immune microenvironment, increasing the risk of disease recurrence or death among patients with solid tumours
6
,
7
.
In blood cancers, the BM microenvironment undergoes profound remodelling, with non-haematopoietic cells reprogrammed to sustain malignant growth
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
. Whether similar microenvironmental alterations emerge during the earliest phases of clonal selection in CH has remained an unresolved question with major implications for understanding malignant evolution. In this Article we model a somatic mutation in the gene DNA methyltransferase 3A (
DNMT3A
) that is frequently observed in CH and acute myeloid leukaemia (AML)
12
to understand the impact of CH on the BM microenvironment.
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis caused transcriptional remodelling of non-haematopoietic cells, including induction of senescence in mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs). Senescence induction occurred in a cell type-dependent manner mediated by the cytokines tumour-necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) produced from
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells. We find that MSC senescence induced by
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis promotes the selective advantage of mutant HSCs over their wild-type counterparts and promotes progression to myeloid malignancy. Furthermore, MSC senescence is broadly observed in human CH, supporting the translational relevance of our observations.

Using these data, we examined the effects of
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis on the wild-type non-haematopoietic BM microenvironment. We observed no differences in the frequencies of non-haematopoietic cell clusters, including MSCs, osteoblasts and subsets of endothelial cells (ECs) (Extended Data Fig.
1d
). Differential expression analyses revealed that MSCs had the greatest number of DEGs from mice with
Dnmt3a
-mutant versus control haematopoiesis (Fig.
1c
). To identify biological programs activated in this context, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on genes significantly upregulated in each non-haematopoietic cell type (FDR < 0.05, log
2
FC > 0). Upregulated genes in MSCs in mice with
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis were uniquely enriched in signatures of senescence, cell cycling, oxidative stress, extracellular matrix, inflammation, and DNA damage and repair, with these senescence-relevant categories absent from or markedly less prominent in osteoblasts and ECs (Fig.
1d
; all significantly enriched GO terms across non-haematopoietic cell types (
n
= 3,227 at FDR < 0.05) are shown in Extended Data Fig.
1g
). To further characterize the senescence-associated transcriptional response, we applied curated gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to the ranked list of upregulated MSC genes and observed significant enrichment of p53 targets, senescence, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) (Fig.
1e
and Supplementary Table
2
). Using an established set of 77 genes upregulated during cellular senescence (FRIDMAN_SENESCENCE_UP)
18
, we calculated their average expression as a composite senescence ‘score’. This senescence score was significantly elevated in MSCs from mice with
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis relative to controls (Fig.
1f
and Supplementary Table
2
) and included the key senescence-associated genes
Cdkn1a
(p21),
Cdkn2a
(p16),
Tp53
and
Mmp14
(Extended Data Fig.
1h
). Thus,
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis is associated with molecular signatures of senescence in BM MSCs.
We next examined whether the same senescence-associated programs are evident in human MSCs in the context of CHIP (clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential). We performed scRNA-seq on BM mononuclear cells from individuals undergoing elective hip replacement, including CHIP carriers harbouring mutations in
DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1
and/or
CCAR1
with VAF > 0.02 and non-carrier controls (Supplementary Table
3
). The MSC population was defined using established markers (
PDGFRA, CXCL12, RUNX2, GHR, CDH11
and
APOE
) consistent with prior single-cell analysis of human BM
19
. We calculated a composite senescence score using the same gene set of 77 genes described above and observed that this was significantly elevated in MSCs from CHIP carriers compared to non-carriers (Fig.
1g
and Supplementary Table
2
) including increased expression of
CDKN2A
(p16) and
MMP14
(Extended Data Fig.
1i
). Thus, MSCs in individuals with CHIP driven by a variety of somatic mutations exhibit similar transcriptional signatures of senescence and SASP, demonstrating a conserved MSC senescence program across species in CH.
Recent reports describe an inflammatory signalling axis from immune cells to stromal cells in the BM that drives clonal evolution in CHIP and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)
20
,
21
. Given the overlap between inflammatory factors and SASP, we used our scRNA-seq data to assess whether MSCs in CH/CHIP were in a state of inflammation rather than senescence. Pre-ranked GSEA of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in mouse MSCs revealed that whereas the SASP signature was significantly enriched in mice with
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis (normalized enrichment score (NES) = +1.96, FDR = 0.004), four independent inflammatory signatures including an inflammatory MSC (iMSC) signature
22
and hallmark inflammatory response, interferon-alpha (IFN-α) response and IFN-γ response gene sets were all significantly depleted (NES range −1.99 to −3.10, FDR < 0.007) (Extended Data Fig.
1j
). Examination of the leading-edge genes driving these results confirmed that the SASP signal was driven by canonical SASP effectors including
Angptl4, Cxcl5, Mmp2, Hgf, Mif
and
Vegfc
, with no overlap with the genes responsible for negative enrichment of inflammatory signatures (Extended Data Fig.
1k
). Key markers of inflammatory MSC identity, including
Irf7
and
Ly6e
23
,
24
, were among the leading-edge genes most strongly depleted in
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis. To validate this dissociation in MSCs in human CHIP, we computed per-sample gene set z-scores for the SASP, iMSC and IFN-γ signatures. The SASP score was significantly elevated in MSCs from humans with CHIP relative to non-carriers (mean z-score +0.2019 versus −0.1731,
P
= 0.0174), whereas neither the iMSC score (
P
= 0.0661) nor the IFN-γ score (
P
= 0.2353) significantly differed between groups (Fig.
1h
). Together, these findings demonstrate that enrichment of senescence and SASP programs in MSCs occurs in the absence of strong inflammatory transcriptional activation, supporting a model of paracrine senescence in the BM microenvironment rather than an inflammatory stromal response.
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis induces cellular senescence in MSCs
There is no single definitive marker for senescence, and analysis of cellular senescence requires a combination of approaches, as recently reinforced by the SenNet Biomarkers Working Group
25
. We developed a multi-parameter framework to identify MSC senescence integrating senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, B-cell lymphoma (BCL)-xL/BCL-2 co-positivity, cell morphological changes and, in selected experiments, senescence-associated gene expression and p16 reporter activity as orthogonal measures (Extended Data Fig.
2
), and validated this approach in established senescence models of ageing and irradiation (Extended Data Fig.
3
). We note that each marker captures a distinct feature of the senescence program. SA-β-gal reflects increased lysosomal activity, BCL-xL/BCL-2 co-positivity marks the anti-apoptotic state characteristic of senescent cells
26
,
27
,
28
, and p16 expression identifies canonical CDK inhibitor-driven senescence. Therefore, the absolute frequency of marked cells differs between assays. Our conclusions accordingly rely on the directional consistency of enrichment across these orthogonal markers rather than on any single absolute estimate. Using this framework, we evaluated senescence in non-haematopoietic cells in the BM microenvironment in mice with
Dnmt3a
-mutant or control haematopoiesis. Steady-state
Dnmt3a
R878H/+
Mx-Cre mice (Fig.
2a
) showed no change in frequency of CD51
+
PDGFRα
+
LepR
+
MSCs (Fig.
2b
; the gating strategy is shown in Extended Data Fig.
2b
), but a greater proportion of these MSCs expressed cellular markers of senescence (approximately threefold increase in SA-β-gal
hi
BCL-xL
+
BCL-2
+
versus control haematopoiesis) (Fig.
2c,d
and Extended Data Fig.
4a–d
) and had increased expression of
Cdkn1a, Cdkn2a
and
Trp53
(Fig.
2e
). In contrast, no changes were observed in ECs isolated from the same mice (Extended Data Fig.
4e–h
), demonstrating cell context specificity of the senescence phenotype. We also evaluated steady-state
Dnmt3a

4i–o
). Thus, induction of
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis using two complementary Cre recombinase models results in MSC senescence.

expression outside of the haematopoietic system. To address this, we restricted

-mutant haematopoiesis had a greater proportion of MSCs expressing senescence markers compared to control haematopoiesis (approximately threefold increase) with no change in ECs (Fig.

and Extended Data Fig.

-mutant haematopoiesis had a greater proportion of senescent MSCs compared to control haematopoiesis (~1.2-fold increase) (Fig.

-mutant haematopoietic cells induce senescence in wild-type MSCs in vivo, even when present at low levels that are physiologically relevant to the clonal burden observed in human CHIP. The functional consequences of this enrichment are assessed directly in the perturbation experiments described in the following.
Our molecular analysis of primary human samples suggested that MSC senescence can be driven by a variety of CHIP somatic mutations. To test whether this is true in mice, we evaluated a mouse model of
TET2
-mutant CH
31
. In steady-state
Tet2

We then asked whether primary human
DNMT3A
-mutant HSPCs induce molecular senescence in primary human wild-type MSCs. We co-cultured MSCs from young donors with CD34
+
HSPCs isolated from older individuals with
DNMT3A
-mutant CHIP or age- and sex-matched individuals without CHIP and performed bulk RNA-seq on the MSC fraction (Fig.
3e
and Supplementary Table
3
). We find that MSCs cultured with CHIP HSPCs were enriched for a robust molecular signature of cellular senescence (SEN_MAYO)
33
(Fig.
3f
and Supplementary Table
2
), and using the same 77 gene set described in Fig.
1
, we observe that the senescence score is significantly elevated in MSCs cultured with CHIP compared to control HSPCs (Fig.
3g
and Supplementary Table
2
). Further assessing senescence at the protein level, we observed that MSCs cultured with CHIP HSPCs had higher levels of SA-β-gal compared to control HSPCs (Fig.
3h,i
). Together,
DNMT3A
-mutant haematopoiesis is sufficient to induce MSC senescence in primary mouse and human cells ex vivo.
To narrow down potential mechanisms of MSC senescence induction, we next asked whether cell contact between mouse
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells and wild-type MSCs is required. Using transwell dishes that physically separate HSPCs and MSCs but allow secreted molecules to diffuse (Extended Data Fig.
6d
), we observed that wild-type MSCs co-cultured with
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells had increased levels of SA-β-gal (Extended Data Fig.
6e
) and
Cdkn1a
expression (Extended Data Fig.
6f
) compared to controls. In a complementary approach, we collected conditioned media (CM) from
Dnmt3a
-mutant or control HSPCs and added this CM to cultures of primary wild-type MSCs (Fig.
3j
). MSCs cultured with CM from
Dnmt3a
-mutant HSPCs had increased levels of SA-β-gal (Fig.
3k–m
) and
Cdkn1a
expression (Fig.
3n
), and were in a state of replicative senescence assessed over seven days (Extended Data Fig.
6g,h
). These data show that
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells induce MSC senescence in a cell contact-independent manner.
To identify soluble factors produced by
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells with the potential to induce MSC senescence, we defined a putative secretome. We cross-referenced DEGs in
Dnmt3a
-mutant HSCs with annotated secreted proteins and proteins detected in HSC/MPP datasets
34
,
35
(Extended Data Fig.
6i
). As inflammatory cytokine transcripts are often under-represented in RNA-seq, we complemented this analysis with a multiplexed enzyme-linked immunoabsorbent assay (ELISA) of
Dnmt3a
-mutant and control HSPC CM, finding that
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells produce elevated levels of IL-6, TNF-α, GDF-15 and osteopontin (OPN) (Fig.
3o
). We then functionally evaluated a subset of these factors (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α) for their ability to induce senescence of primary mouse MSCs. We found that recombinant murine IL-6 and TNF-α were sufficient to induce SA-β-gal in MSCs (Fig.
3p
). We then tested these factors in primary wild-type human MSCs (Supplementary Table
3
), finding that recombinant human TNF-α, but not IL-6, was sufficient to induce SA-β-gal in human MSCs (Fig.
3q
). These data show that soluble factors produced by
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells, particularly TNF-α, are sufficient to induce primary mouse and human MSC senescence.
We then asked what SASP factors are released by senescent MSCs, to determine if these were distinct from senescence-inducing factors such as TNF-α. We induced MSC senescence using
Dnmt3a
-mutant CM, then removed the CM and replaced it with fresh MSC media for four days, followed by media collection to assess potential SASP factors. We observed that senescent MSCs produce elevated levels of the SASP factors MMP3 and S100a9, but not TNF-α (Extended Data Fig.
6j,k
), which distinguishes SASP from factors that induce MSC senescence.
Lastly, we queried the functional impact of CH-driven MSC senescence on wild-type versus
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis. We used
Dnmt3a
-mutant CM to induce senescence of wild-type MSCs, removed the CM and then added wild-type HSPCs for co-culture (Extended Data Fig.
6l
). Wild-type HSPCs exposed to senescent MSCs had reduced proportions of phenotypic HSCs and increased granulocyte–macrophage progenitors (GMPs) (Extended Data Fig.
6m
). Similarly, in vivo induction of MSC senescence by
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis impacted wild-type haematopoiesis, causing a reduced proportion of HSCs and increased GMPs (Extended Data Fig.
6n,o
). In contrast,
Dnmt3a
-mutant HSPCs exposed to senescent MSCs have a greater proportion of phenotypic HSCs and decreased GMPs (Extended Data Fig.
6p,q
). These data suggest that there is a distinct functional response of wild-type versus
Dnmt3a
-mutant HSCs to MSC senescence wherein wild-type HSCs undergo differentiation while mutant HSCs are maintained or expanded.
STAT3 signalling selectively induces senescence in MSCs
We next sought to understand the molecular basis of the cell context specificity of senescence induction in MSCs but not ECs in the context of CH. In other cell types, TNF-α and IL-6 induce senescence through Stat3 signalling and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation
36
,
37
, and targeting Stat3 causes selective death of senescent cells
38
. We hypothesized that ‘senescence-sensitive’ MSCs versus ‘senescence-resistant’ ECs in CH may have different expression levels of TNF-α or IL-6 receptors, and/or differential activation of Stat3 or ROS. In the context of
Dnmt3a
-mutant CH, we observed increased levels of TNF-α receptor 2 (TNFR2) and IL-6 receptor (IL6R) and no change in TNF-α receptor 1 (TNFR1) on MSCs (Fig.
4a,b
), and these all were unchanged in ECs (Fig.
4c
). This same pattern was also observed in steady-state mice with
Dnmt3a
-mutant CH (Extended Data Fig.
7a,b
). With respect to downstream signalling, we found increased phosphorylation of Stat3 (pStat3) and levels of cellular ROS in MSCs in mice with
Dnmt3a
-mutant compared to control haematopoiesis (Fig.
4d
), also with no change in ECs (Fig.
4e
). Further analysis of our scRNA-seq data found that MSCs in
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis had increased signatures of ROS, DNA damage response signalling, and p53 activation compared to control haematopoiesis (Fig.
4f
), and these signatures were not enriched in ECs. We did not observe increased phosphorylation of other potential downstream mediators of TNF-α or IL-6 signalling, including NF-κB, MAPK/ERK and AKT, in MSCs of mice with
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis in either steady state or post-transplant conditions (Extended Data Fig.
7c–e
). Together, TNF-α and IL-6 receptor levels and downstream Stat3 phosphorylation are associated with the selectivity of senescence induction in MSCs in response to
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis.

Discussion
We have demonstrated that
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells directly alter the BM microenvironment and induce senescence selectively in MSCs, both in the context of steady-state ‘native’ haematopoiesis as well as following transplantation. This is found even when the proportion of
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells in the BM is small (equivalent to a CH VAF of 1–4%) as well as in the context of human MSCs isolated from individuals with CHIP caused by multiple driver mutations including
DNMT3A
,
TET2
,
ASXL1
and
CCAR1
. Furthermore, our findings are replicated in a mouse model of
Tet2
-mutant haematopoiesis, supporting that a subset of the most common mutations identified in CH are associated with MSC senescence. Senescence induction does not rely on physical cell-to-cell interaction but instead is caused by secreted factors such as TNF-α, which is produced by mutant haematopoietic cells
49
,
50
,
51
. We find that depletion of senescent non-haematopoietic cells reduces the selective advantage of
Dnmt3a
-mutant HSPCs and their progeny and reduces progression to myeloid neoplasms. The convergent results from three orthogonal senolytic strategies provide causal evidence for this relationship that does not depend on any single estimate of senescent MSC abundance. Although the enrichment of senescence-associated MSCs in the setting of CH is modest in absolute terms—consistent with the minority-population nature of niche regulatory cells—the dose-dependent correlation between MSC senescence levels and
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic fitness observed across individual mice demonstrates that even this quantitative range is functionally consequential. This is conceptually consistent with a recent report that MSC senescence is associated with progression from the precursor condition monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) to multiple myeloma (MM)
52
. Our results establish that CH clones can alter their surrounding BM microenvironment to create a favourable niche that facilitates their competitive advantage and promotes the outgrowth of neoplastic cells.
In mice, TNF-α and IL-6 produced by
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells are necessary and sufficient to induce MSC senescence. Senescence of human MSCs in the context of CHIP may involve an overlapping but distinct set of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α. Further in vivo studies and in-depth profiling of primary human samples will be needed to definitively assess which soluble factors drive human MSC senescence in CHIP. TNF-α signalling has been associated with
DNMT3A
-mutant CH in mouse models
49
and in humans
53
, and elevated TNF-α and IL-6 are observed in
TET2
-mutant CH
54
,
55
,
56
, suggesting that these inflammatory mediators may function within an integrated signalling network that promotes MSC senescence and microenvironment remodelling. Whether MSCs begin expressing inflammatory factors in the context of CH and how this relates to MSC senescence is an important question. A substantial research challenge lies in the overlap of SASP factors with markers of inflammation. In observing SASP enrichment in MSCs in the absence of upregulation of broad inflammatory gene signatures, our data are aligned with an interpretation of MSC senescence rather than MSC inflammation in the context of CH/CHIP. The extent to which this process mechanistically overlaps with the inflammatory signalling axis from immune cells to stromal cells recently reported to drive clonal evolution in CHIP and MDS
20
,
21
remains to be determined.
There are several limitations to our study that should be considered in future work. We administered poly(I:C) to mice carrying the Mx-Cre allele to induce the
Dnmt3a
R878H
mutation. Poly(I:C) is a synthetic double-stranded RNA that induces an inflammatory response, and inflammation is known to drive the selective advantage of CH-mutant haematopoietic cells
49
,
51
,
53
,
55
,
57
. Although we have validated key experiments using the TAM-inducible Fgd5-CreER
Dnmt3a
R878H/+
model, we cannot rule out that some of the phenotypes we report are impacted by poly(I:C)-induced inflammation. Our experiments demonstrate that
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoietic cells induce MSC senescence, but we have not identified the specific haematopoietic cell type(s) that mediate this effect. Furthermore, although our data support a model in which
Dnmt3a
-mutant HSPCs secrete cytokines that initiate MSC senescence, followed by secondary SASP production by senescent MSCs, our study does not definitively delineate the respective contributions of haematopoietic and stromal compartments to the full inflammatory milieu associated with CH. Future studies capable of cell type-specific cytokine perturbation will be necessary to distinguish the stromal versus haematopoietic origins of inflammatory factors and to dissect autocrine versus paracrine mechanisms. Although we performed several control experiments with the p16-3MR mouse model, concerns have been raised regarding the reproducibility and robustness of this model
58
. Furthermore, in administering the senolytics ABT-263 and D + Q to mice systemically, we cannot exclude that they have other effects on haematopoietic or non-haematopoietic cells that contribute to a reduction in
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis or in transformation from
Dnmt3a
-mutant haematopoiesis to myeloid neoplasia. ABT-263 is limited in clinical utility as it causes dose-dependent thrombocytopenia due to BCL-xL dependence in platelets. Importantly, the clinical limitations do not negate the biological insight: demonstration that senolytics can reduce CH provides a proof of concept that the senescent microenvironment is targetable to alter pre-malignant clone dynamics, and it opens the door to safer translational approaches (for example, targeted senolytic delivery to BM, intermittent dosing or platelet-sparing BCL-xL degraders now under development).
In solid tumours, tumour-infiltrating CH-mutant haematopoietic cells remodel the tumour immune microenvironment and increase the risk of disease recurrence and death
6
,
7
. Whether circulating CH-mutant haematopoietic cells induce senescence in other normal and malignant tissues, which soluble factors drive senescence in other tissues, and how this contributes to CH-associated disease states, will be an exciting area of further investigation. Our work provides a clear rationale for testing whether targeting aberrant senescent MSCs can attenuate the haematopoietic and non-haematopoietic sequelae of CH.
Our findings in CH may be relevant to the spectrum of adult tissues that accumulate somatic mutations in the context of ageing. For example, studies in the ageing colon, endometrium, oesophagus and skin show that clonal evolution can occur years or decades prior to the development of malignancies
59
. How the stroma dynamically co-evolves with age in mutated but histologically normal clonal tissues, including induction of senescence, needs further exploration. In preclinical models, interventions targeting senescent cells have been shown to delay, prevent or alleviate multiple disorders, including diabetes, cardiac dysfunction, cognitive impairment and osteoporosis
60
,
61
. Exploring the feedforward interaction between senescence and clonality in different microenvironmental compartments may reveal new therapeutic approaches to disrupt these interactions and attenuate ageing-associated disease.
Together, these findings establish a foundational mechanism through which CH activates senescence programs in MSCs and demonstrate how senescent stromal cells may contribute to clonal fitness. This work provides a platform for future mechanistic studies and highlights potential translational opportunities to intercept CH and reduce the risk of blood cancer and other CH-associated pathologies.

Senescence score computation
A composite senescence score was computed using the FRIDMAN_SENESCENCE_UP gene set
18

74

For bulk human MSC RNA-seq data from co-culture experiments, per-sample scores were calculated as the mean expression value across all signature genes detected in the dataset, without prior per-gene standardization.
GO term biological category classification
To reduce redundancy in the GO enrichment output and facilitate cross-cell-type comparison, all significantly enriched GO terms (FDR < 0.05) were assigned to one of 17 predefined biological categories using a keyword-based classification applied to GO term descriptions. Categories were defined as: General Metabolism & Regulation, Development & Differentiation, Inflammation & Immune Response, Transport & Localization, Transcription & Epigenetics, Vesicle & Secretory Trafficking, Cell Cycle & Proliferation, Apoptosis & Cell Death, Cytoskeleton & Motility, Signalling Pathways, Translation & Ribosome, Energy & Mitochondrial Metabolism, Proteostasis, Lipid Metabolism, DNA Damage & Repair, Oxidative Stress & ROS, and ECM & Cell Adhesion. Each term was assigned to the first matching category in a fixed priority order using a curated keyword list matched against the lower-cased GO term description; terms matching no category were retained as unclassified. All GO terms passing the FDR threshold were included; none were excluded after categorization. Six categories were designated senescence-relevant based on their established roles in senescence and SASP: Senescence & Ageing, Cell Cycle & Proliferation, DNA Damage & Repair, Oxidative Stress & ROS, ECM & Cell Adhesion, and Inflammation & Immune Response. Full category assignments and enrichment statistics are provided in the source data.

Statistics and reproducibility
No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized except where noted above. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during the experiments and outcome assessment except where noted above. For ex vivo imaging experiments, investigators were blinded to mouse genotypes prior to and during data analysis. For mouse experiments, animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups. All statistical tests were performed using Prism 9 software (GraphPad) as described in the figure legends. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. Differences among group means were considered significant when the probability value was <0.05. Sample size (
n
) represents number of biological replicates.
