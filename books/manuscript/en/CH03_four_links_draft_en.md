## 1 | Three frameworks that talk to each other

Readers often ask which theory of aging is right. It is a fair question, and this chapter will not answer it. It asks a different one first: suppose a theory were right. What would that tell us about who is at risk, about what we can change, and about whether changing it helps?

The book's first principle, which it calls I1, is that these are separate steps. Explaining why something happens is not the same as being able to change it; changing it is not the same as benefiting the person; and benefit, even when it is real, does not entitle anyone to decide for others what a good life is. This chapter adds one more step that readers meet constantly: prediction, which sits between explanation and control. A marker can tell us who is at risk without telling us what to do about it.

Consider two recent frameworks from one laboratory. In "Simply aging" (2026), Gladyshev argues that aging is a default outcome of living: because biological processes are inherently imperfect, deleterious changes, which he calls the deleteriome, accumulate over time and erode functional reserve. The deleteriome is meant to cover molecular damage, regulatory drift, maladaptive remodelling and system-level failure without reducing aging to any one gene, pathway, hallmark or pathology. A review in Nature the same year, by Yücel, Molière and Gladyshev, proposes something narrower: that aging reflects a progressive loosening of cell identity, because a regulatory grammar written during development with high fidelity can afterwards be maintained only by local mechanisms with limited fidelity. As support, it points out that the vast majority of age-associated gains in DNA methylation in dividing tissues occur at regions bound by PRC2, and it offers testable predictions for interventions that stabilise this architecture.[^1]

A third framework comes from evolutionary biology. In a 2024 essay, Lemaître and colleagues note that why and how we age are intertwined questions, and that attempts to answer them remain compartmentalised. They argue that two evolutionary theories involving physiology, the disposable soma theory and the developmental theory of aging, lack clarity about how they relate to the evolutionary genetic theories of mutation accumulation and antagonistic pleiotropy. They propose a hierarchical model linking genes to vital rates, meant to generate testable hypotheses about how natural selection shapes the hallmarks of aging.[^2]

These frameworks talk to each other. The first two share an author and a phrase: in both, maintenance works with limited fidelity. The third asks why natural selection would leave maintenance imperfect at all. But they are not one theory. The deleteriome is an accounting frame, broad enough to absorb almost any harmful change. Identity drift names a specific structure and a specific pattern in the data. The evolutionary framework asks about the forces that shaped both.

Breadth has a cost. If every change that goes with a bad outcome can be counted, after the fact, as part of the harmful burden, then "function declined because the burden grew" can become a redescription rather than an explanation. A framework earns more trust when it says in advance which result would count against it: which burden, measured where and how, should change which function, and by how much. Explaining a great deal is not the same as ruling something out beforehand. That test applies to all three, and to the frameworks this book will itself propose.

## 2 | Three words that cannot lend each other units

Three words travel through writing about aging as if they were interchangeable: entropy, information and control.

Entropy has a precise meaning in physics, and living things do not violate it: they are open systems that use energy to keep local order. So the fact that entropy increases overall does not show that any particular tissue cannot be repaired. Nor does the fact that order costs energy show that supplying energy would stop aging. A fibrous scar can be more ordered than the tissue it replaced and still work worse. "Disorder" and "damage" are not the same quantity.

Information needs a carrier, an error model and a task before it means anything. A DNA sequence, a pattern of chromatin, the spatial layout of a tissue and a person's memories can all be described as information, but they are not one substance measured in one unit. A framework that speaks of "lost information" owes the reader an answer to the question: information about what, stored where, read by whom?

Cell identity and personal identity are the sharpest case. The first is a cell's lineage and functional state. The second involves the continuity of a body, memory, relationships, commitments and legal standing. A cell whose expression profile looks younger has not shown that the self has been preserved, and keeping a memory intact does not show that a tissue is safe. Both kinds of identity can constrain a technology, but there is no formula that unites them. Chapter 14 returns to the question "who is getting younger?"

The words matter even inside one paper. The same 2026 essay says that biological age is, in some contexts, partially reversible or resettable, although aging itself is irreversible. Both halves can be true only if "biological age" and "aging" name different things: the first an estimate that, as the essay puts it, modern biomarkers make imperfectly but increasingly measurable; the second the process. A headline that reads "aging reversed" off a reading that moved has swapped one for the other.[^1]

Control, finally, is not omniscience. In engineering it means steering a system with limited observation and limited authority, and above all avoiding errors that amplify. The useful questions are modest: what must be kept, what can be replaced, what is reversible, and can we stop if something goes wrong?

## 3 | Prediction without control, and control without benefit

Start with prediction. A marker predicts an outcome when people with different values have different risks. That is valuable: it can show who needs closer attention. It does not show that changing the marker would change the risk.

A small synthetic calculation, built by this project with no real data, shows how this can happen. Take 1000 people, 300 of whom have a hidden condition. The marker is high in 80 percent of those with the condition and in 20 percent of those without; the event happens to 40 percent of those with the condition and 10 percent of those without, whatever their marker. Among the 380 people with a high marker, 110 have the event, a risk of 0.289; among the 620 with a low marker, 80 do, a risk of 0.129. People with a high marker have more than twice the risk, a risk ratio of 2.24. Now give everyone a perfectly safe treatment that sets the marker low. The number of events stays at 190.

The marker in that calculation is an excellent predictor and a useless target, because it has no effect of its own. Real cases are rarely so clean, but the pattern is real. In a 2012 study of HDL cholesterol, observational data showed that each standard deviation higher was associated with fewer heart attacks, an odds ratio of 0.62. The same study then used mendelian randomisation, which relies on the fact that genotypes are assigned at random at meiosis and are not changed by disease, to treat genetic variants that raise HDL cholesterol as a natural experiment. A 1 standard deviation increase due to a genetic score was not associated with risk of heart attack (odds ratio 0.93, 95 percent interval 0.68 to 1.26). The authors concluded that some genetic mechanisms that raise HDL cholesterol do not seem to lower risk of myocardial infarction, which challenges the idea that raising it will uniformly translate into lower risk.[^3]

Control without benefit is the next gap. Torcetrapib, a potent CETP inhibitor, was tested in 15,067 patients at high cardiovascular risk to see whether it would reduce major cardiovascular events. At 12 months it had raised HDL cholesterol by 72.1 percent and lowered LDL cholesterol by 24.9 percent; it had also raised systolic blood pressure by 5.4 mm Hg. The risk of cardiovascular events was higher (hazard ratio 1.25), and so was death from any cause (hazard ratio 1.58, 95 percent confidence interval 1.14 to 2.19). The trial was stopped early. The authors called the mechanism of harm unknown: there was evidence of an off-target effect, but they could not rule out harm from CETP inhibition itself.[^4]

The Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial shows the same gap with a mechanism that seemed close to the outcome. It was designed to test the hypothesis that suppressing extra beats from the ventricles after a heart attack would reduce sudden death. Only patients whose extra beats could be suppressed by encainide, flecainide or moricizine were randomly assigned to active drug or placebo, so control of the marker was achieved before randomisation. Of 1498 patients in the encainide and flecainide comparisons, after a mean of 10 months, 43 on these drugs had died of arrhythmia against 16 on placebo, and the two drugs were discontinued because of excess mortality. The authors wrote that the mechanisms behind the excess deaths remained unknown.[^4]

The ACCORD trial moved a marker with a documented link to the outcome. Epidemiological studies had shown a relationship between glycated haemoglobin and cardiovascular events in type 2 diabetes. The trial assigned 10,251 patients either to intensive therapy targeting a level below 6.0 percent or to standard therapy targeting 7.0 to 7.9 percent. The control worked: at one year the median levels were 6.4 and 7.5 percent. But 257 people died in the intensive group, against 203 in the standard group (hazard ratio 1.22, 95 percent interval 1.01 to 1.46). Over 3.5 years, intensive therapy increased mortality and did not significantly reduce major cardiovascular events.[^4]

Here is the strongest objection to this chapter, and it deserves a full answer: a good enough mechanistic theory would collapse the four steps. If we really understood how a disease works, prediction, control and benefit would follow together. Sometimes they do line up. The same 2012 study included a positive control: for LDL cholesterol, the observational estimate (an odds ratio of 1.54 per standard deviation) agreed with the genetic one (2.13). Here prediction and causal evidence point the same way.[^3]

But lining up is something to be shown, not assumed. In the arrhythmia trial and the torcetrapib trial, the mechanistic story was good enough to justify a large trial, and each trial still found harm that its own authors could not explain. In ACCORD, the link between the marker and the outcome was well documented, and pushing the marker further still did not deliver. A better theory narrows which trials are worth running. It does not replace them, because a body is not a single pathway: a treatment acts on everything it touches, and the outcome counts all of it. Chapter 18 treats these cases, and the general problem of markers and outcomes parting ways, in detail.

## 4 | Benefit is not deciding for others what a good life is

Suppose every gap so far were closed: the explanation is right, the marker is causal, the treatment changes it, and people who take the treatment live longer or function better on average. One step remains, and it is not scientific.

A benefit measured in a trial is an average over people who agreed to take part, counted in the outcomes the trial chose. For a particular person, the same treatment comes with its own burdens: side effects, time, cost, monitoring, and the way it reshapes days. Some people will accept a large burden for a small gain in survival. Others will not, and they are not making a mistake. Chapter 1 called this the question of who decides what is worth it, and chapter 2 showed that even a health-adjusted statistic carries weights someone chose.

So this book separates two claims that are often fused. "This intervention, in these people, changed this outcome by this much" is an empirical claim, and the book tries to state it exactly. "Therefore you should want it" is not an empirical claim at all. It depends on what the person values, which the evidence cannot supply and the author has no standing to impose. The book keeps the body's maintenance and the person's opportunities as two ledgers that are not added into one score; chapter 30 makes that argument in full.

None of this is relativism about evidence. A treatment that increases deaths is not made acceptable by anyone's preferences, and a well-measured benefit is not diminished because someone declines it. The point is narrower: evidence can tell a person what is likely to happen. It cannot tell them what to want.

## 5 | What this means for the rest of the book

The separation runs through every later chapter. Chapter 4 asks what counts as evidence for any of the four steps. Chapter 5 sets out a contract a measurement has to meet before it can be used for prediction, for tracking change, for mechanism or as a stand-in for an outcome. Chapters 6 to 9 take the four kinds of measurement in turn: level, rate, recovery and reserve. Volume Ⅱ asks, when a reading changes, what actually changed. Volume Ⅲ returns to judgement: what is worth maintaining, and who decides.

The book also proposes ideas of its own, and the same rules apply to them. The project keeps a list of candidate shifts in how aging research might frame its questions. They are research syntheses, offered as examples of sharper questions, not as discoveries or new paradigms, and each has to earn its place by predicting something an existing framework does not.

This book is not exempt from prior work either. Its later chapters lean on ideas about capacity and queues, and those ideas have predecessors: Ledberg derived the Gompertz law from queueing theory, and the ledger also lists a model by Karin and Alon and work on critical slowing and dynamic resilience. The project's own ledger records this and asks the book to describe its contribution as recombination and identifiability, not as a first (ledger X85).[^5]

Knowing why is worth a great deal. It tells us where to look and which experiments are worth doing. It does not, by itself, tell anyone what to do.
