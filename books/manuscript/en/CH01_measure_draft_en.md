## 1 | Two people and a concert

Imagine two people of the same age who both want to go to a concert next week. One is worried about the walk from the car park to the hall. The other needs someone to come along and wants to know whether a wheelchair can get through the entrance. Both might wish their bodies were stronger. But their problem is not only in their muscles. Transport, the building, the cost, company, and whether they decide for themselves where to go all help decide whether the evening happens.

This is an invented scene, not a description of two real patients. Nor does it make walking unaided the price of a life worth living. For someone who has a carer, uses a wheelchair or lives with an illness, a concert, a friendship or a decision of their own can still be worth a great deal. Bodily capacity matters, and so do support and surroundings. Neither fully replaces the other.

If longevity research asked only "how many more years?", the scene would be beside the point. If it asked only "how young does a test say I am?", it would still be beside the point. But if we care about what people can do in a finite life, what happens to them, and how they live with others, then every layer, from molecules to the entrance of a concert hall, can be part of a question about longevity.

That is why this book's title uses the word *measure* in three senses, and keeps them apart rather than playing on them. The first is **measurement**: readings, tests and the numbers they produce. The second is **the measure of what can be changed**: the limits of an intervention, what it can and cannot alter. The third is **the measure of a life**: judgments about what is worth having and worth doing. A number can be precise in the first sense and say nothing in the other two. Much confusion about longevity comes from letting one sense stand in for another.

The book is organised around five questions that connect the three. This chapter introduces them one at a time.

## 2 | What is the extra time?

Begin with the strongest objection to everything that follows. Someone might say: survival is the main good. Talk of quality of life distracts from preventing early death, and a book about measures should not dilute that. This objection deserves to be granted in full. Death cannot be undone. Avoiding an early death can let a person see a child grow up, finish work they care about, or simply have another ordinary morning. Every other thing this book discusses depends on being alive to have it. For many people, one more day is reason enough for a choice. Nothing in this book argues that more years count for less than better ones, or that a measure of quality should be traded against survival without the say of the person whose survival it is.

Granting that does not settle what a statistic about time means. Life expectancy is built from the death rates of a particular population in a particular period. Health-adjusted life expectancy goes further and weights each year of life by an estimate of health. A 2024 study used World Health Organization data for its 183 member states. It reported that the gap between the two, which the authors call the healthspan–lifespan gap, had widened over the previous two decades to 9.6 years, that women's gap was on average 2.4 years wider than men's, and that the United States had the largest gap, at 12.4 years.[^1] This project's reading of the full text found that the paper reports both a global figure and an average across countries, and that the two are not interchangeable (ledger X21). Chapter 2, which owns these statistics, takes up the difference.

What does a gap of 9.6 years mean for one person? Less than it seems. A small synthetic calculation, built by this project with no real data, makes the point. Imagine three populations of 100 people. In all three, life expectancy is 18 years, health-adjusted life expectancy is 15 years, and the gap is 3 years. In the first, 20 people die at 10 and 80 at 20: nobody dies at 18, and nobody has exactly 3 lost years; people have either 1 or 3.5. In the second, all 100 die at 18 and every one of them has exactly 3 lost years. In the third, deaths are as in the first, but in the second decade 20 of the survivors are in the worst health state throughout while 60 are in full health: 80 people have 1 lost year and 20 have 11. Three very different sets of lives share the same three summary numbers.[^2]

The arithmetic is deliberately simple, and it shows two things. An average life expectancy is not an appointment: it is a property of a population, not a date in anyone's diary. And the gap is a count of *equivalent* years, years weighted by health, not a forecast that every person will spend that many years ill. The summary cannot say whether the burden is spread thinly over everyone or falls heavily on a few. That difference matters, both for the people concerned and for anyone deciding what to do about it.

Health-adjusted measures are useful precisely because they stop us looking only at length. They are also limited, because the weights cannot capture how a person feels, the support around them, or what they value. Illness and disability can bring pain, barriers and unfair burdens, and they deserve serious prevention and treatment. But a life with illness or disability does not lose its meaning on that account. A statistical loss of health is not a verdict on the worth of anyone's life.

## 3 | Who, and what, is being maintained?

The body is not a machine waiting for spare parts, but it does need maintaining. Wounds have to heal, bones have to bear load, the immune system has to tell threats from self, and people need to recover after illness. An intervention can improve one of these and cost another. Seeing a cost is not a reason to refuse an intervention. It is a reason to put benefits and harms on the same table.

One trial shows why "did it work?" can have more than one answer. In the CALERIE trial, adults without obesity were randomly assigned either to a target of 25 percent calorie restriction or to their usual diet for two years. A later analysis of DNA methylation in blood found that the intervention slowed the pace of aging as measured by one algorithm, DunedinPACE, but did not significantly change biological-age estimates from other clocks, including PhenoAge and GrimAge, and that the treatment effects were small; two of its authors are listed as inventors of DunedinPACE, which was licensed to a commercial company. A report on bone found that, compared with the usual-diet group, the restriction group had greater losses of bone mineral density at 24 months at the lumbar spine, the total hip and the femoral neck; its authors called bone loss at these sites of osteoporotic fractures a potential limitation of prolonged restriction, and wrote that longer studies are needed to find out whether it contributes to fracture risk. A safety report concluded that two years of restriction at the levels achieved in the trial was safe and well tolerated, and that close monitoring for excessive bone loss and anemia is important.[^3]

These are three reports from one trial, not three confirmations. They do not even count the same people: the methylation report describes 220 randomized adults, the bone report 218. This chapter cannot say why. Reports from one study often differ in who is counted. But it means the reports cannot be combined into a portrait of any one participant. Whether the people whose clock readings slowed are the people whose bones thinned is not something the published averages can tell us (ledger X29). And 25 percent was a target. The safety report's own phrase, "at levels achieved in CALERIE", is a reminder that what people were asked to do and what they did are different quantities.

None of this shows that eating less is dangerous, and none of it shows that it is beneficial. A drop in bone density is not yet a fracture, and a slower clock reading is not yet a longer life. What the case does is turn one question into four: what improved, what may have been harmed, for how long, and who bears the cost?

"Who" does not stop at the person. A blood reading can change because cells changed inside, or because the mix of cells in the sample changed (chapter 6). Some cells can be replaced, and an organ may work better or carry a new cost. Whether a change deserves the word "younger" depends on whether we mean a cell, a lineage of cells, an organ or a person, and on what was actually tracked. Without knowing which cells were measured before and after, a study can report a reading, but it cannot claim that each original cell went back in time. Chapter 15 develops this question of identity.

## 4 | What is seen, and what is missed?

An instrument does not give a full view of the body. It gives one view, after sampling and processing. Blood is easy to collect, but that does not mean every organ moves in step with the blood. The people who stay in a study and attend every visit leave records; that does not mean the people who left had the same experience. How much a number can be trusted depends on more than its decimal places. It depends on what was measured, and on what was not seen.

One example recurs in this book. A trial may first assign people at random to a programme and only later sort them by what actually happened to them: who lost weight and who did not, who took every dose and who stopped. The random assignment protects the comparison between the groups as assigned. It does not make the later groups random. Illness, circumstances, behaviour and many unrecorded things can shape both who ends up in a later group and how they fare. Statistical adjustment helps, but it cannot guarantee that all of this has been removed. Such analyses can be worth doing. They should not be read as the trial that was never run.

None of this means that every gap makes a study worthless. Researchers can define in advance what they want to estimate, work hard to follow people up, report who was lost, and test whether their conclusions survive different assumptions. More detailed measurement can remove ambiguity, though each new measure brings its own questions: is it accurate, does it apply to the same people, and what burden does it add? Chapter 4 turns these into reading habits, and chapter 5 gathers them into a short contract that any number can be asked to sign.

## 5 | Is a change already a benefit?

A grab rail does not change a person's date of birth, but it can change how they get in and out of the bath. A drug can change a molecular pathway without improving anything a patient cares about. For research, "how does it work?", "can it change the reading?" and "does it bring net benefit?" are different questions. For the person concerned, net benefit also includes side effects, time, cost, access and their own preferences. Chapter 3 owns this separation.

A randomized trial in Baltimore, Maryland, shows a change that happens outside the cell. It enrolled 300 low-income adults aged 65 or older, living in the community and reporting difficulty with daily activities. Participants were assigned either to up to ten home visits over five months by occupational therapists, registered nurses and home modifiers, working on functional goals the participants chose themselves, or to ten social visits by a research assistant. At five months, the trial reported a 30 percent reduction in scores for difficulty with basic activities of daily living (relative risk 0.70, 95 percent confidence interval 0.54 to 0.93), and a smaller reduction for instrumental activities of daily living that was not statistically significant (0.83, 0.65 to 1.06). Two of the authors are inventors of the programme's training, for which their university is entitled to fees.[^4]

That result is worth taking seriously, and it is easy to over-read. Each task was self-scored from 0 to 2 and the total ran from 0 to 16, so the 0.70 compares disability scores between the groups; it is not a 30 percent lower risk of any event for each person. The programme combined several things, so the result cannot be divided between the home modification, the nurse and the therapist. Participants were interviewed again at twelve months, but this chapter relied on the trial's abstract, which reports the five-month end point; the later result is listed below as unread. And a five-month change in difficulty with daily tasks is not evidence of a longer life, which the trial did not claim. What it does show is that if the question is whether someone can do the things they value, an intervention need not happen inside a cell. Chapter 23 owns housing and environment as interventions.

Caution is not another word for doing nothing. For some well-evaluated interventions, waiting has its own cost. For new, high-risk methods whose long-term results are unknown, smaller promises and rigorous trials may fit better. Each decision needs its own evidence and the person's own circumstances, not one rule for everyone.

## 6 | Who decides what is worth it?

This book keeps the meaning of a longer life in two accounts that cannot be converted into each other. One is bodily maintenance: pain, disease, function and survival. The other is life opportunity: autonomy, relationships, an accessible world, and how a person wants to use their time. The two affect each other, but they should not be compressed into one score that ranks everyone's lives for them. This is the book's value position, not a law of nature measured in a trial. Chapter 30 develops the two accounts.

There is a serious objection. Public budgets are limited. Without converting benefits and costs into a common measure, it is hard to compare treatment with housing or care. That objection has weight. A common measure can make the assumptions behind spending decisions public, and can stop decisions being made by whoever shouts loudest. But whatever summary is used, its weights should be published. They should be checked for whether they undervalue the lives of disabled people, the costs carried by carers, or services that disadvantaged groups find hard to reach. And the people affected should be able to take part in revising them. Needing to make trade-offs does not give any single number the right to hide them.

Preferences also conflict. Some people would accept intensive treatment for a few more months. Others value time in familiar surroundings more. Autonomy does not mean that everyone must carry their choices alone. It means being able to understand the options, get support, and change one's mind. Care and interdependence are not the opposite of autonomy; reliable support is often part of it. Chapter 24 owns care, dependence and autonomy.

## 7 | Carrying the five questions forward

This book returns again and again to five questions:

1. **What is the extra time?** How long, measured how, and for whom?
2. **Who, and what, is being maintained?** Which capacity, in which cell, organ or person, and at what cost elsewhere?
3. **What is seen, and what is missed?** What was measured, in whom, and who dropped out of view?
4. **Is a change already a benefit?** Did a reading move, or did a life improve?
5. **Who decides what is worth it?** Whose values, and with what say for the people affected?

They are not an exam to be answered in order. They are a set of checks that constrain one another. A promising molecular mechanism still has to face questions of function and safety. A programme that eases daily life still has to ask whom it suits, whether it lasts, and whether people can get it fairly. A judgment about a good life has to admit that others may disagree. The first three questions belong mostly to *measure* in its first sense, measurement; the fourth to its second sense, what can be changed; the fifth to its third, what is worth having. Each chapter that follows uses its own short list of checks for a particular kind of number. Those lists serve these five questions; they do not replace them.

The book moves from time, the body and evidence, through cells, tissues and environments, and back to human choices. It will not write every unknown as "there is no answer". Nor will it let a report about being younger erase the complexity of illness, disability, care and relationships. The measures of longevity are demands on our readings, reminders of the limits of what we can change, and an open question about how we live together.
