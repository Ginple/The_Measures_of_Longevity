Two people are both fifty, and both receive the same "biological age" on a report: forty-seven. For one of them, that number has hardly moved in ten years of annual tests. For the other, it was thirty-nine ten years ago. The two reports are identical. The two people are not. One is holding steady; the other is changing fast, in the wrong direction, and happens to be passing through the same value on the way.

Chapter 6 ended with the question most people actually ask of a longevity number: "Am I aging more slowly?" That is a question about a **rate**: how fast a level changes over time, within the same person. This chapter is about what it takes to measure one, and about the shortcuts that look like rates but are not.

## 1 | Same level, different slopes

A level is a point. A rate is a slope, and a slope needs at least two points from the same person, measured the same way. That sounds obvious, yet much of what is said about the speed of aging rests on something else: one measurement per person, compared across people of different ages.

One of the clearest demonstrations of within-person rates comes from a birth cohort in New Zealand. Researchers followed 954 members of the Dunedin Study, tracking multiple biomarkers at three time points across their twenties and thirties, and built a longitudinal measure of the pace of coordinated decline across organ systems, including lung, gum, heart and blood vessels, kidney, liver and immune function; young people of the same calendar age differed in how fast they were aging, and, before midlife, those aging faster were less physically able, showed cognitive decline and brain aging, rated their own health as worse, and looked older.[^1]

What makes this a measurement of rate rather than of level is the design. Members of the cohort were born within about a year of one another, so calendar age was nearly the same for all of them at each visit; what differed was how much each person's biomarkers changed between visits. A single visit could have shown who looked older. Only repeated visits could show who was getting older faster.

## 2 | A difference between age groups is not a change within a person

The cheaper way to estimate a rate is to measure many people once, line them up by age, and draw a line through the cloud. The slope of that line is a statement about differences between age groups. It is often read as a statement about how one person changes over time. The two are not the same, and the gap between them can be large.

Telomeres again provide a simple example. In the meta-analysis met in chapter 6, which pooled 414 study samples, the median rate of telomere change estimated from cross-sectional comparisons was about 23 base pairs per year, while the median rate measured longitudinally, by following the same people, was about 38 base pairs per year.[^2] The two designs answer different questions, and here the cross-sectional one made shortening look slower. The same analysis found that the rate itself was not constant across life: shortening slowed gradually until around age 50 and then stayed relatively stable.

The same pattern appears for a quantity that matters directly for daily life: peak aerobic capacity, the most oxygen a person can use during hard exercise. Researchers measured it repeatedly in 375 women and 435 men aged 21 to 87, from a community cohort without clinical heart disease, over a median of 7.9 years; capacity fell in every age decade in both sexes, but the rate of decline accelerated from 3 to 6 percent per ten years in the twenties and thirties to more than 20 percent per ten years in the seventies and beyond, and the authors warned that rates estimated mainly from cross-sectional studies may be misleading and overly optimistic.[^3]

Why would a cross-sectional slope be too gentle? Part of the answer is who is still in the sample. A seventy-five-year-old who can walk into a laboratory and run on a treadmill is not a typical seventy-five-year-old from that birth year; people whose capacity fell fastest are more likely to have died, become ill, or stopped taking part. Comparing them with today's thirty-year-olds also compares people who grew up in different decades, with different diets, work and medical care. Neither problem is a flaw in any one study. Both are built into the design. This is reasoning about the comparison, not a result reported by the studies cited here.

## 3 | When the rate itself changes

If aging proceeded at a steady speed, one slope per person would be enough. It may not. The aerobic capacity data above already show a rate that speeds up with age. Two recent studies suggest that molecular change in blood may also come in bursts rather than at a constant pace.

A Stanford group profiled many kinds of molecules in a longitudinal cohort of 108 Californians aged 25 to 75, followed for a median of 1.7 years and at most 6.8 years, and reported consistent nonlinear patterns, with substantial dysregulation clustered around two periods, at roughly 44 and 60 years of age.[^4]

A separate group in Singapore analyzed 3.8 million blood immune cells, one cell at a time, from 1,828 people aged 19 to 97, of whom 35 percent were of Asian ancestry; differences in gene activity peaked around age 40, mainly in one type of T cell (CD4), and after 60, mainly in another (CD8), with a timing that differed between women and men; aging clocks trained separately for each sex performed better than combined models.[^5]

Two groups, two populations and two kinds of measurement pointing to transitions in the forties and around sixty is more persuasive than either alone. But notice what kind of evidence it is. A median follow-up of 1.7 years cannot show one person passing through both forty-four and sixty. In the first study, the age pattern across fifty years comes mostly from comparing people of different ages, and the second abstract describes a comparison across 1,828 individuals without mentioning repeated samples from the same people. These are the cross-sectional comparisons of section 2, with all their limits. They are good reasons to doubt that "the rate of aging" is a single number per person. They do not yet show when any one person's rate changes.

## 4 | A pace clock is a level that estimates a rate

Measuring a rate the hard way takes years. A tempting shortcut is a test that estimates the rate from one sample. That is what a "pace of aging" clock tries to do.

The best-known example was built from the Dunedin cohort itself. Its developers modeled each person's decline in 19 indicators of organ-system integrity across four visits spanning two decades, then trained a single-time-point blood test of DNA methylation to predict that two-decade pace, using only probes that had passed a test-retest reliability filter; they reported that the resulting measure, DunedinPACE, showed high test-retest reliability, was associated with illness, disability and death, and indicated faster aging in young adults who had experienced adversity in childhood. Several of the developers are named inventors on a university invention that was licensed to a commercial company.[^6]

Two things follow. First, a pace clock is a level: one sample, one reading. It is a level trained to predict a rate that was measured, in one cohort, the slow way. Whether it tracks the rate of a different person, in a different population, after an intervention, is a separate question that the training cannot answer. Second, the strength of the design comes from the cohort's long within-person follow-up, which is rare and valuable; the same feature means the target is the pace of decline of New Zealanders born in 1972–73, measured with their particular set of biomarkers.

Can a pace clock respond to an intervention? In a randomized trial in which 220 adults without obesity were assigned to a 25 percent reduction in calorie intake or to their usual diet for two years, a post hoc analysis of blood samples found that the intervention slowed the pace of aging as measured by DunedinPACE, but did not significantly change several other DNA methylation clocks, including PhenoAge and GrimAge; the authors described the treatment effects as small. The analysis was not part of the trial's original plan, and two of its authors are inventors of DunedinPACE.[^7]

Two further details belong next to that result. The 25 percent figure was the prescribed target; the restriction participants actually achieved was much smaller and fell over time. And the methylation analysis covered fewer people than the 220 who were randomized. Both points are recorded in this project's evidence ledger.[^8]

Is responsiveness common? A Yale-led group assembled 51 public and private longitudinal intervention studies into one database, computed 16 prominent epigenetic clocks and 94 other DNA methylation measures consistently across them, and found that clocks trained to predict mortality or pace of aging showed the strongest responses across interventions and agreed with one another, and that the study population and the length of the study were key factors in whether a measure responded; the authors frame the work as a step toward finding methylation measures that could one day serve as surrogate endpoints. Three of the authors are employees of TruDiagnostic and developed one of the measures studied; two are co-inventors of another, which has been patented in the United States; and two have received consulting fees from TruDiagnostic.[^9]

The same group also asked how stable these readings are when nothing deliberate has changed. Across 18 methylation-based aging measures, most showed excellent technical reproducibility when the same material was run again, though some were sensitive to laboratory details such as the position of a sample on the slide and the DNA extraction method; but when blood was collected repeatedly over short intervals, under ordinary variation such as meals, stress and environmental exposures, stability was substantially lower, low to moderate for most clocks, and lower still after adjusting for immune-cell composition; technical reproducibility did not predict this biological reliability.[^10]

Put those results together and the question in this chapter's opening, "My pace score dropped; am I aging more slowly?", has a careful answer. A pace clock is among the readings most likely to move after an intervention. That is useful for designing trials. It is not the same as showing that the intervention changed the course of aging. Chapter 5 kept these properties apart: responding to an intervention is one property; being a validated surrogate, a reading whose change reliably predicts the change in clinical outcomes, is another. The second has not been shown for any methylation clock.

There is also simple arithmetic to keep in mind. The numbers in this paragraph are illustrative, not taken from any study. Suppose a reading wobbles from week to week with a standard deviation of 0.05 units around a person's true value. The difference between two independent readings then has a standard deviation of about 0.07 units, because the wobble of both readings adds up. A single drop of 0.05 between two tests would then be entirely ordinary, even if nothing about the person had changed. A change in one person becomes informative when it is large compared with this wobble, when it persists across several readings, or when it is compared with a control group measured in the same way.

## 5 | Same sample, different software, different number

A rate is a difference between two readings. If the two readings are not made in the same way, the difference may be a difference in method.

Clocks are software as well as chemistry. The publicly released code that computes DunedinPACE, in the version this project pinned, documents how it handles missing data: if a probe's values are missing for some samples but present for enough of them (by default, 80 percent), the gaps are filled with the average of the samples submitted together; if too many are missing, every sample's value for that probe is replaced with the average from the training data; data from a newer array version are detected automatically and the missing-data threshold is lowered from 80 to 70 percent; and, because of how missing data are handled, the code's own documentation recommends running entire cohorts at once.[^11]

None of this is a defect. It is a reasonable way to handle imperfect data, and it is documented. But it means a person's score can depend, slightly, on which other samples were processed in the same batch, and on which array generation measured the sample. Two readings taken years apart, on different arrays, in different batches, possibly with a different software version, can differ for reasons unrelated to the person. A published trial that used this clock linked to the official code repository without stating which version it ran, and this project has not been able to establish it.[^12] That is not evidence of any error in the trial. It is the kind of detail that decides whether two readings can be subtracted.

## 6 | What a rate claim needs

A rate can carry something a level cannot: direction and speed. It is also harder to measure honestly. Before accepting the sentence "your aging has slowed", it helps to ask five questions.

Were there at least two readings from the same person, or is the "rate" a comparison across different people of different ages? Were the readings made the same way: same kind of sample, same laboratory, same array generation, same software version and processing? Is the change large compared with how much the reading moves from week to week when nothing has changed? Was there a comparison group measured the same way over the same period? And is the reading a measured slope, or a single reading trained to estimate one?

Even a well-measured slope describes the past interval. It says how fast a reading moved, not how much room is left before something fails. A slow decline that starts close to a threshold can matter more than a fast one that starts far away. Before that, though, there is a third quantity to separate: how a system responds when it is pushed and then released. That is the subject of chapter 8, on recovery. Distance from the threshold is the subject of chapter 9.
