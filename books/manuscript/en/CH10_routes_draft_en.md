## 1 | Five operations, not a ladder

Two questions come up whenever longevity science is discussed. One reader asks whether the goal is to make the body young again. Another, often caring for a parent, asks whether a walking frame, a hearing aid or a paid carer counts as doing anything about aging at all. The two questions assume a ladder: real treatments at the top, which turn the body back, and mere help at the bottom, which only works around it.

This book does not use that ladder. It sorts interventions by what they try to change, into five operations:

| Operation | What it tries to change | The main risk question | A success that still does not show a younger body |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maintain | Slow the production of damage; keep regulation within a workable range | Does long-term suppression cost repair, immunity or nutrition? | Lower risk of one disease |
| Repair or clear | Mend damage; remove harmful molecules or cells | How selective is the removal, and what is left where something was removed? | Better function of one tissue |
| Reset | Change a cell's state or its regulatory settings | Loss of identity, abnormal growth, delivery, stopping | A change in one clock or one local function |
| Replace | Put a new component where the old one no longer works | Integration, immune response, durability, supply, access | A prosthesis or graft that restores one function |
| Compensate | Rebuild the route to a service or an action without removing the cause | Dependence, continuing upkeep, burden, fragile interfaces | Real support with no change in biological age |

The operations are not exclusive. A hip replacement replaces a joint, and the physiotherapy afterwards maintains the muscles around it. Nor are they stages of maturity, as if compensation were what medicine does until it learns to reset. Each route has its own way of failing, listed in the third column, and its own way of being misread, listed in the fourth. Chapter 19 takes up resetting and clearing in detail, and chapter 11 the costs of damage and repair. This chapter is about the last column: what success on each route does and does not show.

## 2 | What success on each route does not show

The key sentence of this chapter is short. A system does not need to be young at every level to be more reliable at one task that matters. The converse also holds: recovering a task does not mean the original damage has gone.

Lowering blood pressure is maintenance in this sense. In the SPRINT trial, 9361 people with a systolic blood pressure of 130 mm Hg or higher and an increased cardiovascular risk, but without diabetes, were randomly assigned to a target below 120 mm Hg or below 140 mm Hg. After a year, their average systolic pressures were 121.4 and 136.2 mm Hg. The trial was stopped early, after a median of 3.26 years, because the primary composite outcome of heart attack, other acute coronary syndromes, stroke, heart failure or cardiovascular death was less frequent in the intensive group: 1.65 percent a year against 2.19 percent, a hazard ratio of 0.75. Deaths from any cause were also lower, with a hazard ratio of 0.73. Serious adverse events of low blood pressure, fainting, electrolyte abnormalities and acute kidney injury were more frequent with intensive treatment, though injurious falls were not.[^1] The trial measured events, deaths and harms. It did not measure whether anyone's arteries became younger, and it did not need to: a route can work on the outcome that matters without any claim about age.

Replacement is the clearest case of restoring a task without reversing a process. In a randomised trial, 100 people with moderate-to-severe knee osteoarthritis who were eligible for a knee replacement either had the operation followed by 12 weeks of nonsurgical treatment, or received the 12 weeks of nonsurgical treatment alone: exercise, education, dietary advice, insoles and pain medication. After 12 months, the surgical group had improved more on a combined score of pain, symptoms, daily activities and quality of life that runs from 0 (worst) to 100 (best): 32.5 points against 16.0, an adjusted difference of 15.8. The surgical group also had more serious adverse events, 24 against 6. And 13 of the people assigned to nonsurgical treatment, 26 percent, had the operation anyway before 12 months.[^2] The new knee does not make the rest of the body younger, and the trial is a reminder that the comparison for a replacement is not "nothing" but the best available alternative, with its own gains.

Compensation is often judged against a question it was never designed to answer. Hearing loss is associated with faster cognitive decline, and the ACHIEVE trial asked whether treating it would slow that decline. Adults aged 70 to 84 with untreated hearing loss and no substantial cognitive impairment were randomly assigned to audiological counselling and hearing aids (490 people) or to health education sessions (487). The primary result was a null: over three years, cognitive change was the same in both groups, with a difference of 0.002 standard deviations (p=0.96). A prespecified sensitivity analysis found that the effect differed between the two populations the trial recruited from, one drawn from a long-running study of cardiovascular health, whose members were older and had more risk factors for cognitive decline, and the other made up of new community volunteers, and the authors suggested that hearing treatment might slow decline in people at higher risk.[^3] That suggestion is a lead, not a result. The point here is narrower. A hearing aid compensates for hearing. Whether it also changes the brain's course is a second question, and a null on the second question says nothing against the first. The trial's declarations are worth reading as well: hearing technologies used in the study were donated in kind by Sonova/Phonak, and the lead author directs a research centre funded in part by a philanthropic donation from the company Cochlear. Chapter 4's habit applies: the interest is declared, and the primary result went against the hoped-for effect.

## 3 | Durability has two sources

A treatment that works while it is being given raises a second question: what happens when it stops? There are two quite different ways for a benefit to last. The system itself can be left in a changed state, so that the benefit persists after the treatment ends. Or the benefit can depend on support that has to keep being supplied.

A mouse study published in 2025 makes the difference concrete. Its authors compared young and aged mice across the central and peripheral niches of the immune system, and identified two signalling pathways, Notch and FLT3L, together with the signal IL-7, as declining with age. They then delivered messenger RNA encoding three of these signals, DLL1, FLT3L and IL-7, to liver cells in aged mice. The liver was repurposed, temporarily, to restore immune cues that diminish with age. The treatment expanded the early cells from which lymphocytes develop, increased the production of new T cells in the thymus without changing the composition of blood stem cells, and replenished T cells. In aged mice it improved responses to a peptide vaccine and restored immune responses against tumours.[^4]

Two things in this abstract need stating precisely. First, the thymus was not bypassed: one of the reported effects is more new T cells made in the thymus. The liver supplied signals; it did not replace a young thymus. Second, the effects were reversible after dosing ceased, and did not breach self-tolerance, in contrast to the inflammatory and autoimmune problems of recombinant cytokine treatments.[^5] Reversibility cuts both ways. It means the treatment can be controlled and stopped. It also means the benefit, on this evidence, lasts only as long as the support is supplied. An absence of autoimmunity in the period studied in mice does not show the safety of repeated dosing over years, and better vaccine and tumour responses are not the same as protection against the full range of infections, or a longer life. Two of the authors are co-inventors on a patent application relating to the work.[^5]

Neither source of durability is better in itself. A lasting change in the system is not automatically safer: a change that cannot be undone cannot be undone when it goes wrong. Continuing support is not automatically lesser: a great deal of medicine, from insulin to dialysis, works this way. What matters is that the two are not confused. "Effective while taken" and "a permanent repair" are different claims, and a report that does not say what happened after stopping has not said which one it is making.

## 4 | Compensation is not second-class

The strongest objection to this chapter is that only fixing root causes counts, and compensation merely hides aging. Part of this is right. Compensation can create dependence. It needs continuing upkeep, and its interfaces can be fragile: a hearing aid with a flat battery, a ramp that a new landlord removes, a carer who leaves. A person whose life rests on many such supports is exposed to every one of them failing.

But the objection treats biological age as the only outcome. For the person, the outcome is often a task: getting out of bed, bathing, cooking, going out. In the CAPABLE trial, 300 low-income adults aged 65 or older with a disability, living in their own homes in Baltimore, were randomly assigned to up to 10 home visits over 5 months from occupational therapists, registered nurses and home modifiers, who worked on goals the participants chose themselves by building their capacity and changing their homes, or to 10 social visits from a research assistant. At 5 months, scores for difficulty with basic activities of daily living were 30 percent lower in the CAPABLE group (relative risk 0.70). The difference in instrumental activities of daily living was a 17 percent reduction that was not statistically significant.[^6] Nothing in the trial claims to have changed anyone's biological age. Difficulty with tasks fell, and for the people concerned that may be the result that matters most.

The same trial shows why compensation deserves the same scrutiny as any other route, not less. The two inventors of the CAPABLE training programme are among the authors, and their university is entitled to fees from it.[^6] And the project's audit of the trial's records found that the study's design paper listed basic activities of daily living as the primary outcome and instrumental activities as secondary; the current trial registration lists only the basic activities; and the 2019 results paper lists both as primary. Without the full protocol and analysis plan, the audit cannot judge whether this is a violation, an error or a bias, or in which direction.[^7] It can only record that the description of what the trial was mainly testing differs between documents. Chapter 1 used this trial to show how easily a relative score is over-read; here it shows something else. A route being humane does not exempt its evidence from being checked.

## 5 | What to report for any route

The five routes lead to one reporting rule. For any intervention, on any route, a report should say:

- **During treatment:** what changed, in whom, measured how, and against what comparison.
- **After stopping:** what happened when the treatment ended, and how long people were followed. If no one was followed after stopping, the report should say so, because it then cannot tell a lasting change from continuing support.
- **The burden of keeping it up:** what the person has to do, take, attend or pay for the benefit to continue, and what happens if one part of the support fails.

None of the three can be folded into a single score. An earlier version of this project tried to write maintenance capacity as a formula, a product of terms with subtractions. The project's own review withdrew it: no variable, unit or interaction was defined, and nothing could be calculated from it.[^8] Maintenance capacity survives in this book as a research concept with several parts, taken up in chapter 21, not as a number.

What this chapter does not answer is which route works best for whom. That depends on the person, the task and the evidence for each route, and it is the subject of chapter 20 and, for decisions under uncertainty, chapter 29. What it offers instead is a set of questions that work for all five: what did the route try to change, what does success on it not show, and what happens when it stops.
