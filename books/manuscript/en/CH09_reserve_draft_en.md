A man of eighty climbs the stairs to his flat every day. He is a little slower than he was, but he manages, and his doctor's numbers look reasonable for his age. What none of those numbers says is how much slack is left: how far his capacity could fall before the stairs, the shopping and the walk to the bus stop stop being possible. Two men who manage the same stairs today may be very differently placed. One has room to spare. The other is one bad winter away from needing help.

This chapter is about **reserve**: the distance between where a capacity stands now and the threshold below which a system can no longer do what is asked of it. Chapter 6 dealt with level, chapter 7 with rate and chapter 8 with recovery after a push. Reserve brings in something the other three leave implicit: the threshold. A distance can only be measured once both ends are named.

## 1 | A resting reading can look normal while the room is gone

The idea is not new. In 1960, Strehler and Mildvan published a theory of mortality in *Science*. Later authors describe it as one of a family of models that represent death as the loss of survival capacity through challenges and dissipation: capacity wears down with age, and death comes when a challenge exceeds what is left.[^1] We have not read the 1960 paper itself; this description is secondhand. The modern contribution of this chapter is narrower: to show how reserve can be measured in people, and why converting it into years depends on choices that are easy to hide.

The kidney gives a clear organ-level picture. Its filtering capacity at rest is measured as the glomerular filtration rate. Ask it to work harder, for example with an infusion of amino acids, and in a healthy kidney the rate rises. The size of that rise is often called renal functional reserve.

In a small study, researchers measured filtration before and after an amino acid infusion in 15 healthy young adults with a median age of 26 and in 10 healthy older adults with a median age of 70. At rest, the older group's median was lower, 102 against 122 mL/min per 1.73 m², but virtually all of the older people's values were still within the normal range. After the infusion, filtration rose significantly in both groups: the median reached 118 in the older group and 146 in the younger one.[^2]

Two things stand out. First, a resting test would have called the older kidneys normal. Second, after the push, the older group's median was still below the younger group's median at rest. The median rise was about 16 units in the older group against about 24 in the younger (a difference of medians, not a median of differences), so in this small sample the extra capacity on demand was smaller as well as starting lower. The abstract available to us is cut off before the authors' conclusions; as background, it notes that this reserve is impaired in aged rats. Ten older volunteers cannot carry a general claim. But the shape of the finding is the shape of this chapter: a normal resting reading and a reduced distance to the limit can sit side by side.

## 2 | Where is the threshold?

For whole-body function, the most studied capacity is aerobic: the most oxygen a person can use during hard exercise, measured on a treadmill and usually expressed per kilogram of body weight.

One research group measured it in 298 people aged 55 to 86, 152 men and 146 women, drawn as a random sample of people living independently. They fitted a straight-line decline with age, though age explained at most 37 percent of the differences between people. The decline relative to body weight was 0.31 units per year in men and 0.25 in women, slower than reported for younger groups. And they estimated the minimum aerobic power compatible with an independent life at age 85: approximately 18 mL/kg/min in men and 15 in women.[^3]

Those two numbers are the closest thing the literature offers to a threshold for independent living. It matters how they were obtained. Everyone in the sample was, by design, living independently. So the figures describe the lowest capacities among people who were still managing, not a point at which anyone was observed to lose independence. A true failure threshold could be lower, could be higher for people with other illnesses, and is surely a band rather than a line. A threshold drawn from the edge of a group of survivors is an estimate with a direction of bias, and the book treats it that way.

A brief review takes the same threshold values, says they are reached at around 80 to 85 years, and argues that regular aerobic exercise can slow or reverse the decline, reducing biological age by 10 or more years and potentially prolonging independence by a similar amount. The same review notes that the importance of falling aerobic fitness, relative to other causes of dependency, still needs to be clarified.[^4] The threshold, in other words, is useful and real, but it is not the only way to lose independence.

## 3 | Turning distance into years needs a rate and a model

Reserve becomes something people care about when it is converted into time: how many years until the threshold? That conversion needs a rate of decline, and the rate depends on how it was measured.

The sources disagree in an instructive way. The study of 298 people, which compared people of different ages at one time, found the decline was slower in old age than in younger groups. The review just cited says the rate seen in middle age continues unabated after retirement. And the longitudinal study used in chapter 7, which measured the same 810 people repeatedly over a median of 7.9 years, found that the rate accelerated from 3 to 6 percent per ten years in the twenties and thirties to more than 20 percent per ten years in the seventies and beyond, and warned that cross-sectional estimates may be misleading and overly optimistic.[^5] Three sources, three shapes: slowing, steady and accelerating. The first comes from comparing different people, and the last from following the same people, which is the stronger design for measuring a rate.

To see what this does to an answer, the project wrote a short synthetic calculation, using the published values above as illustrative inputs. It is arithmetic, not an observation or a prediction for anyone. Take a man with an aerobic capacity of 25 and a threshold of 18. If decline is a straight line of 0.31 units per year, as in the cross-sectional study, he has 22.6 years before reaching the threshold. If instead decline is proportional, at the rate implied by "more than 20 percent per ten years", he has at most 14.7 years. ("At most", because "more than 20 percent" sets only a lower limit on the rate.) For a man starting at 20, the two answers are 6.5 and 4.7 years.[^6]

The same choice governs claims about what a gain is worth. In the same arithmetic, raising this man's capacity from 25 to 35 adds 32.3 years before the threshold under the straight-line model, and at most 15.1 years under the proportional one.[^6] Neither number should be read as the effect of training: the calculation assumes a fixed threshold, a smooth decline with no illness, and a gain that lasts. The point is that a statement such as "this could keep you independent for ten more years" cannot be checked until the decline model behind it is stated.

## 4 | Near the threshold, level can matter more than rate

The proportional model has a property that runs against a common intuition. Interventions are often judged by whether they slow the rate of aging. But how much a slower rate is worth depends on how far away the threshold is.

In the synthetic calculation, raising the level by 10 percent adds the same 4.3 years whatever the starting distance from the threshold. Slowing the rate by 10 percent adds one ninth of the remaining years: about half a year for the man starting at 20, and 1.6 years for the man starting at 25. The two are worth the same only when the threshold is about 38 years away, far more room than any older adult in these examples has.[^6] For someone close to the threshold, the arithmetic favours raising the level.

This is algebra under stated assumptions, not a finding about any treatment. Real declines are uneven, thresholds are bands, and illness arrives in shocks. The lesson is about questions, not answers: a claim that something "slows aging" says nothing yet about how many years of function it buys, because that depends on where a person stands relative to the threshold.

## 5 | Four measures, one framework

The strongest objection to this chapter is that reserve is just level under another name. If the threshold were fixed and known, that would be close to true: the distance would be the level minus a constant. Reserve earns its place for three reasons that the examples above have shown. It requires naming a threshold and saying how that threshold was set. It can differ from the resting level, as the kidney shows, because capacity under load is a separate measurement. And it can only be turned into time with a rate and a model.

The four measures of chapters 6 to 9 belong together. A large Canadian study of 302,467 people receiving home care, with a median age of 81, found five distinct trajectories of intrinsic capacity and reported that the starting level alone did not fully capture what happened next: people who started at a moderate level were admitted to long-term care more often than people who started low and improved, 48.4 percent against 38.1 percent.[^7] That is an observational comparison of groups defined by a statistical model, and it does not show that improving capacity would prevent admission. But it illustrates the general point: where someone starts and which way they are heading are separate pieces of information.

The project's synthetic model, used in chapters 6 and 8, shows how far this can go. With published human parameter values, two hypothetical interventions were matched so that the average reading among 70-year-old survivors was the same. At one setting, life expectancy also came out the same, 74.43 against 74.42 years. Their survival curves still crossed: one left more people alive at 70, the other more at 90. Only the shape of the curve, the late-life slope or the speed of recovery could tell them apart.[^8] Which intervention is better depends on whether the goal is survival to 70 or to 90, and that is exactly the kind of choice a measure has to state before it can be used.

So, before accepting a statement about reserve, run five checks. What is the threshold, and how was it set: from observed failures, or from the lower edge of people still coping? Under what load was capacity measured, at rest or on demand? What rate of decline was assumed, and was it measured by following the same people? What model turns distance into years? And for whom do the figures hold?

A single reading at one time cannot answer these questions on its own. Level says where a system stands; rate says how fast it moves; recovery says how it comes back after a push; reserve says how far it is from the point where it can no longer cope. Each needs its own measurement, and none of them is a parameter of the body unless a stated model makes it one. Even a well-measured reserve may not be fully usable: capacity that exists in a test can be out of reach in daily life, a problem taken up in chapter 22. Volume Ⅱ turns from measuring these quantities to asking what produces and consumes them.
